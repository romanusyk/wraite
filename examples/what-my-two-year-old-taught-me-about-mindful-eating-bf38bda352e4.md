# What My Two-Year-Old Taught Me About Mindful Eating

> How a toddler’s natural wisdom forced me to face my own mistakes — and one lesson I hope to leave her with.

My two-year-old daughter and I have had very different experiences with food. If there is one person between us who can teach the other some positive nutrition habits, it is definitely not me. In fact, I’ve already learned many lessons from her attitude towards food, particularly during the first few months when she started eating solid food.

With each new ingredient added to her diet, my daughter would scrutinise every morsel, then swallow with genuine awe and satisfaction. Never have I seen a better illustration of what it means to “eat mindfully”.

What was even more surprising to me was how a few moments after relishing her meal she could realise she was done. By contrast, I usually stop eating only a few minutes after I feel completely full.

On top of that, I wish I could refuse food that doesn’t appeal to me as easily as my daughter can. As a child, I was told it was rude to reject any food my mother cooked. Since she cooked all my meals back then, I learned to accept everything on my plate. I’ve never managed to eradicate this belief.

My daughter has such an innate intuition around food. Because these instincts seem so natural and universal, I must have had them too at her age. Even though it is impossible to relive that experience, I’ve tried to put myself in my daughter’s shoes. I’ve shifted my diet towards simple and healthy food — the kind that makes my child so excited. Almost two years later, this way of eating still feels comfortable and delicious, and I have no intention of going back.

## Back to Basics

From childhood, I hadn’t bothered having my diet in the palm of my hand. Insulin and caffeine peaks and troughs had been common for me, especially at the office, where I had seen no reason to turn down all those types of biscuits and snacks always carefully provided for us. Healthy and tasty had been two mutually exclusive descriptions of a meal until my wife showed me that they could coexist.

When I prepared to become a father, I read a few things about baby nutrition. The most surprising revelation was that the pillars of adult flavour — salt, sugar, and fats — are restricted in a baby’s diet. As I’ve always wanted to give my child the best, it felt unfair to feed her only “bland” food.

That “tasteless” food was actually very simple: just whole vegetables, meat, or grains. Still, this type of food was non-trivial to cook. Partly because it wasn’t convenient for my wife to cook two separate meals, but mostly out of genuine interest, we sometimes shared her diet: no sugar, no salt, and no sauces.

The new diet felt savourless at first, but after only a few days I got used to the subtler flavours. By discovering familiar ingredients in a whole new way, I started to enjoy this new approach. Never have I tasted boiled carrots so sweet or broccoli so delicious. It turned out that fewer additives could make the food more palatable.

Although we haven’t given up on sugar and salt entirely, our diet has become much healthier since our daughter was born. More importantly, all three of us usually have the same meals. Well, maybe not entirely. Sometimes, my daughter is upset when she is allowed to have only one biscuit while the rest of the pack goes to her father. I’ve never actually learned to justify this unfairness to her or to myself. As she loves fruit, I started to copy my daughter and get my sugar fix from fresh fruit rather than baked goods. While I can’t say I’ve ever disliked fruit, I had never given it the appreciation it deserves. And I must admit: even though it is not a direct replacement, biscuits don’t seem quite as appealing to me after a plate of fruit.

I’m truly proud of how versatile and balanced our daughter’s diet is. Considering how passionate about food she’s been, this feels like a natural progression. That said, many parents are not entirely happy with their children’s diets. If our parental approach has played a role, all the credit belongs to my wife’s dedication.

She researched countless creative ways to select, cook, and serve healthy food and has remained persistent in following them. Every time we have a meal, I feel grateful to my wife because I see how excited our daughter is about food. Many people told me how proud I would be of my child’s future talents, but they never mentioned that my wife would also show such incredible ones of her own.

In her first two years, my daughter has tasted most adult foods and managed to incorporate them into her diet. Even so, nutrition allurements like pastries and snacks emerge as we grow. I wish my own relationship with these temptations was different; however, my own mistakes might actually benefit my daughter. This article is based on my personal experience, so it may not apply to everyone. Still, I hope it helps my daughter learn to evade those snares in good time.

## When Food Becomes Complicated

My wife and I try to keep our daughter’s diet healthy for as long as possible. Even so, the older she gets, the more curious she becomes about ”grown-up” foods. The most pervasive ingredient in these is sugar. With a mum who loves baking sourdough and a dad who can’t stop eating biscuits until his coffee is gone, our two-year-old is slowly but persistently winning the battle to be given sweet treats.

I can already notice the detrimental effect sugar is having on my daughter’s natural instincts. With sweets, it becomes more difficult for her to control herself. A year ago, we could offer her a plate of various ingredients, and she would naturally select a balanced meal. Now, she might eat a large portion of freshly baked bread and reject her regular meal entirely. Sugar makes a healthy diet much harder to maintain.

Sugar is not the only factor at play. While my daughter is just beginning to understand her emotions, I’ve had plenty of opportunity to observe how my mental state affects my eating habits. After all, “stressed” spelled backwards is “desserts”. Whenever I have a rough day at work, the desire for something sweet becomes strong. Sometimes I give in, sometimes I resist; either way, my emotions clearly influence my relationship with food.

Another lesson my daughter has yet to learn is that adults don’t necessarily regulate their intake based on hunger only. Often, adults believe that others know what and how much they should eat better than they do. As silly as it sounds, this idea has a significant impact. From fad diets to beauty ideals, grown-ups have invented many forms of social pressure and stigma.

Social pressure can appear even in events where the ultimate purpose is relaxation. In pre-covid times, we often had pizza Fridays at our office. When the boxes of all imaginable flavour just arrived, everyone would patiently try a slice of each kind, even though some might have skipped lunch, looking forward to their evening of camaraderie. Now they felt like starving animals yet didn’t want to look like one.

Then, once everyone had taken the edge off their hunger but plenty of pizza still remained, I usually noticed two distinct types of demeanour. While some of my colleagues would relish the food as much as they desired, others seemed to consider this inappropriate. They acted as if we all had the exact same nutritional needs, and that there was a single, universally appropriate amount of food for everyone. If we behaved a little bit more like children, far fewer slices of delicious pizza would end up in the bin.

___

Undoubtedly, the work environment has had a noticeable impact on my eating habits. At the same time, my own approach to food has always been intertwined with how others perceive nutrition — be they my parents, friends, colleagues, or social media influencers. This entanglement makes it difficult to understand why I have my current habits. Comparing my past to my current self is impossible: it always seems like each habit has been part of me for my whole life.

This is why observing my daughter’s attitude towards food is so enlightening: she hasn’t been through so much of this social influence yet. Understanding child nutrition and following some of its patterns gave me a second chance. I was able to honestly examine my current habits from this new perspective. Not all of them are bad; nevertheless, this experience has significantly improved my relationship with food.

Unlike many other skills, healthy food habits are something I should help my daughter retain, not acquire. With my own habits so shaped by the past, this responsibility is far from simple. If I manage to avoid passing on my own shortcomings, I will consider my task a success. I’ve already had many chances to apply the knowledge I gained while preparing to become a parent; yet I have one essential skill left to master — knowing when to stand back.
